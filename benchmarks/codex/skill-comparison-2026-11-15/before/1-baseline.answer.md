**Decision memo — revise before production approval.** Six replicas across three zones provide useful redundancy, but the proposed settings do not establish five-replica availability, safe request draining, or database durability. The following decisions distinguish documented behavior from configuration choices and prerequisites. Citations refer only to the supplied local documentation; ranges are inclusive.

1. **PDB and rolling updates.** `minAvailable: 5` cannot prevent node-failure disruption; involuntary losses merely count against the budget. During a node drain using the Eviction API, the PDB constrains voluntary evictions: with six healthy replicas, one can be disrupted, and further eviction can wait for recovery. Direct deletion bypasses that protection. Deployment rolling updates also bypass PDB enforcement, although their unavailable Pods count against the budget. Thus `maxUnavailable: 2` permits rollout availability to fall to four. Choose `maxUnavailable: 1`, `maxSurge: 1`; alternatively choose zero unavailable with one surge for greater rollout protection, provided scheduling capacity exists. Neither protects against concurrent failures.  
   Sources: `docs/concepts/workloads/pods/disruptions.md`, lines 99–136; `docs/concepts/workloads/controllers/deployment.md`, lines 1191–1211.

2. **Zone distribution.** Configure a topology spread constraint selecting all API replicas, with `topologyKey: topology.kubernetes.io/zone` and `maxSkew: 1`. The key identifies node-label domains. Under `DoNotSchedule`, skew limits the difference between the target domain’s matching-Pod count and the global minimum; violating placements remain unscheduled. `ScheduleAnyway` instead prefers placements reducing skew without enforcing that bound. Six replicas can therefore target two per zone, subject to eligible nodes and capacity.

   Choose the zone-loss tradeoff explicitly: `minDomains: 3` with hard spreading treats the global minimum as zero when fewer than three domains remain eligible, potentially blocking replacements. Omitting it defaults to one, allowing balancing across remaining eligible domains. Failed-zone eligibility depends on affinity and taint policies; redistribution is not automatic. Losing two replicas still leaves four, and removal can leave an imbalanced distribution.  
   Sources: `docs/concepts/scheduling-eviction/topology-spread-constraints.md`, lines 82–130, 169–185, 605–619.

3. **Probes for slow startup.** Use a startup probe with a failure budget sized from measured startup duration. It suppresses readiness and liveness probes until it succeeds. Then use readiness to establish whether the API can accept requests, including required database availability, and liveness to detect an unhealthy application requiring restart. Avoid making database outages alone trigger liveness failures. Readiness failure removes the Pod from matching Service endpoints while leaving its container running; startup or liveness failures reaching their thresholds cause termination and restart according to restart policy. These mechanisms do not establish instantaneous traffic withdrawal.  
   Sources: `docs/concepts/workloads/pods/pod-lifecycle.md`, lines 479–497, 512–528, 544–557; `docs/tasks/configure-pod-container/configure-liveness-readiness-startup-probes.md`, lines 412–421.

4. **Termination and long requests.** The grace-period countdown starts **before** `preStop`; the hook consumes that budget. Kubelet runs the hook before requesting TERM for the container’s main process. A still-running hook can receive a one-off two-second extension; remaining processes receive SIGKILL after grace expires. Concurrently, terminating endpoints remain represented in EndpointSlices with `terminating` status and `ready: false`, excluding them from regular load-balancer traffic; `serving` supports draining decisions.

   Thirty seconds guarantees no request-completion outcome. Implement application shutdown that stops accepting work and finishes outstanding requests, then size grace for hook execution and measured drain time. Validate the actual traffic path.  
   Sources: `docs/reference/kubernetes-api/workload-resources/pod-v1.md`, line 1008; `docs/concepts/workloads/pods/pod-lifecycle.md`, lines 591–639.

5. **Resource settings and utilization HPA.** CPU requests inform placement and provide the denominator for utilization-based scaling; they are not CPU ceilings. Containers can exceed requests when resources permit. CPU limits impose throttling, whereas memory limits are enforced reactively through potential OOM kills, rather than equivalent throttling. Set measured CPU and memory requests, then select limits against latency and memory behavior. A limit may become the request when no request or admission default exists.

   If relevant CPU requests are missing from some containers, Pod CPU utilization is undefined and HPA takes no action for that metric. Inspect effective admitted specifications, including sidecars.  
   Sources: `docs/concepts/configuration/manage-resources-containers.md`, lines 18–53, 64–66; `docs/tasks/run-application/horizontal-pod-autoscale.md`, lines 89–102.

6. **HPA’s conservative calculation.** The raw recommendation is the rounded-up product of current replicas and current-to-target metric ratio. HPA ignores terminating Pods, discards failed Pods, and sets aside missing metrics and certain not-yet-ready CPU samples. Missing metrics are assumed at 100% of target for downscaling and zero for upscaling; not-yet-ready Pods also dampen upscaling through a zero assumption. If recomputation reverses direction or enters tolerance, scaling stops.

   Defaults include 10% tolerance, a 30-second initial-readiness delay, a five-minute CPU initialization period, and five-minute downscale stabilization choosing the highest recent recommendation. Consequently, reported utilization and actual scaling can differ from the raw ratio. A working metrics API is a prerequisite.  
   Source: `docs/tasks/run-application/horizontal-pod-autoscale.md`, lines 113–116, 135–216.

7. **Least-privilege networking.** Establish namespace-wide ingress and egress default denial, then allow the actual entry tier to API ports, API egress to the database port, and matching database ingress from API Pods. Add only required dependencies. Ingress and egress isolation operate independently; policies selecting a Pod combine additively, so a broad allow policy can defeat intended restrictions. Both source egress and destination ingress must permit a Pod connection; replies are implicitly allowed.

   Explicitly allow DNS to the cluster’s actual resolver, including UDP/TCP 53 where applicable. Confirm CNI enforcement: creating policy objects without a supporting implementation has no effect. Service address translation also requires validation.  
   Sources: `docs/concepts/services-networking/network-policies.md`, lines 47–81, 193–206, 254–262; `docs/tasks/administer-cluster/dns-debugging-resolution.md`, lines 158–174.

8. **Secrets and Git.** Reject base64 as protection: the docs demonstrate decoding it and describe encoded credentials as “obscured but not secret.” Remove readable credentials from Git and select a protected delivery mechanism. Enable Secret encryption at rest; Secrets are otherwise stored unencrypted in etcd. Apply least-privilege RBAC and expose credentials only to containers needing them.

   Restrict workload creation as well as direct Secret access: permission to create Pods, including indirectly through Deployments, permits reading namespace Secrets. Encryption at rest does not resolve that authorization exposure or protect credentials already committed to Git.  
   Source: `docs/concepts/configuration/secret.md`, lines 36–48, 282–306.

9. **Storage reclamation and lifecycle.** A StorageClass supplies reclaim policy during dynamic provisioning; changing its policy does not retrofit existing PVs. Inspect and edit existing PV policies individually. `Retain` leaves the released PV and underlying data for manual reclamation after PVC deletion. With supporting plugins, `Delete` removes both PV and backing storage; dynamically provisioned volumes default to this policy.

   PVC deletion differs from StatefulSet deletion or scaling. StatefulSet claim retention defaults to `Retain`; optional `whenDeleted` and `whenScaled: Delete` policies delete affected template-created claims after Pods terminate. Their documented feature-gate prerequisites must be checked. These claim-lifecycle choices are separate from the PV’s subsequent reclaim action.  
   Sources: `docs/concepts/storage/storage-classes.md`, lines 34–36, 128–135; `docs/concepts/storage/persistent-volumes.md`, lines 180–210; `docs/concepts/workloads/controllers/statefulset.md`, lines 391–423.

10. **Prioritized rollout and validation.** First, remove Git credential exposure, establish encryption/RBAC, and verify network-policy enforcement and allowed flows. Second, inspect database PV reclaim and StatefulSet claim-retention settings before destructive lifecycle tests. Third, deploy measured requests, startup/readiness/liveness probes, application draining, compatible rollout limits, and explicit zone-loss policy. Finally, exercise rolling updates, PDB-respecting drains, zone loss, missing metrics, and sustained load.

   Validate at least these unresolved limits: actual startup duration; longest-request completion and routing convergence; capacity for surge and zone-loss replacements; CPU’s relationship to service demand; and database recovery and storage behavior. The documents specify mechanisms, not this application’s latency, capacity, or durability outcomes.  
   Supporting constraints: `docs/concepts/configuration/manage-resources-containers.md`, lines 232–241; `docs/concepts/workloads/pods/pod-lifecycle.md`, lines 619–639; `docs/concepts/scheduling-eviction/topology-spread-constraints.md`, lines 605–619; `docs/tasks/run-application/horizontal-pod-autoscale.md`, lines 153–216.
