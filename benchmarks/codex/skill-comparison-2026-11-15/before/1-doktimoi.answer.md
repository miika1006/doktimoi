**Decision memo — revise before production approval.** The proposed settings do not establish five-replica availability, request completion, or database durability. Adopt the safeguards below, then validate application and cluster behavior. 1. **PDB and rollout availability.** `minAvailable: 5` cannot prevent involuntary node failure; those losses count against the budget. For node drain, eviction requests respect the PDB and can be rejected and retried, so draining may stall until replacement Pods become healthy. This protection requires eviction-based tooling: direct deletion bypasses PDBs. Deployment rolling updates count against the budget but are **not constrained by it**. At six desired replicas, `maxUnavailable: 2` therefore permits four available replicas. Recommend `maxUnavailable: 0, maxSurge: 1`, with capacity for surge and terminating Pods; `maxUnavailable: 1` is also compatible with a nominal five-replica rollout floor. Neither configuration guarantees that floor during simultaneous failures. Sources: `concepts/workloads/pods/disruptions.md:63–66,92–143`; `concepts/workloads/controllers/deployment.md:1183–1212,327–332`.

2. **Zone placement.** Explicitly configure `topologySpreadConstraints` with the API’s label selector, `topologyKey: topology.kubernetes.io/zone`, and `maxSkew: 1`, targeting two replicas per zone when feasible. `topologyKey` groups equally labeled nodes into domains. With `DoNotSchedule`, skew limits the difference between the target domain’s matching-Pod count and the global minimum; violating placements remain unscheduled. `ScheduleAnyway` instead prefers reduced skew while allowing scheduling. Choose strict spreading only after testing failure recovery: an unavailable zone can block replacement scheduling if its domain remains counted. Eligibility depends on affinity and taint policies; `minDomains: 3` makes the global minimum zero when fewer than three domains qualify, potentially blocking further placement. Soft spreading favors recovery capacity over strict balance. Neither rebalancing after removals nor topology discovery without existing nodes is guaranteed. Sources: `concepts/scheduling-eviction/topology-spread-constraints.md:76–133,167–194,605–623`.

3. **Slow-start probes.** Use a startup probe whose `failureThreshold × periodSeconds` covers measured worst-case initialization. Until startup succeeds, readiness and liveness probes do not run. Exhausting startup failures kills the container, subject to its restart policy. After startup, readiness should indicate whether the instance can accept requests; becoming unready removes it from Service load balancers without requiring a restart. Liveness should detect an unrecoverable condition such as deadlock and trigger restart after its configured failure threshold. Avoid making transient database trouble alone a liveness failure: the documented warning about cascading restarts supports this application-specific choice. Probe endpoints and thresholds require measurement, not merely valid YAML. Sources: `tasks/configure-pod-container/configure-liveness-readiness-startup-probes.md:15–48,311–347`; `reference/kubernetes-api/workload-resources/pod-v1.md:1018–1028`.

4. **Termination and long requests.** The grace-period countdown begins **before** `preStop`; the hook consumes that budget. During normal graceful shutdown, kubelet runs the hook, then requests TERM delivery to process 1. A still-running hook can receive a one-off two-second extension. When grace expires, remaining processes receive SIGKILL. Concurrently, terminating Service endpoints remain represented temporarily with `terminating` status and `ready: false`, excluding regular traffic; `serving` supports draining decisions. These are concurrent mechanisms, not a guaranteed synchronous cutoff. Thirty seconds is a default, **not a request-completion guarantee**. Implement draining and TERM handling, then size grace for hook execution and measured outstanding-request completion. Sources: `reference/kubernetes-api/workload-resources/pod-v1.md:1005–1007`; `concepts/workloads/pods/pod-lifecycle.md:584–645`.

5. **Resources and CPU autoscaling.** Specify realistic CPU requests for every relevant container: requests inform scheduling and provide the denominator for CPU-utilization HPA. Requests are not consumption ceilings. CPU limits enforce throttling; memory limits can cause reactive OOM termination rather than CPU-style throttling, and exceeding memory does not necessarily produce an immediate kill. Choose limits through load testing, especially for startup and long-request latency. If relevant CPU requests are missing, Pod utilization is undefined and HPA takes no action for that metric. Inspect effective configuration: when a limit exists without a request or admission default, Kubernetes copies the limit into the request. Sources: `concepts/configuration/manage-resources-containers.md:19–67`; `tasks/run-application/horizontal-pod-autoscale.md:89–102`.

6. **HPA’s conservative adjustments.** The basic calculation rounds up current replicas multiplied by the current-to-target metric ratio, but the controller then adjusts it. Missing-metric Pods are assumed at 100% of target during downscaling and 0% during upscaling, damping changes. CPU metrics for not-yet-ready Pods, including samples preceding readiness, are set aside; if otherwise scaling up, those Pods are conservatively assumed at 0%. A recalculated ratio that reverses direction or falls within tolerance produces no scaling. Defaults include 10% tolerance, a 30-second initial-readiness delay, and a five-minute CPU-initialization period. Downscale stabilization normally selects the highest recommendation over 300 seconds. Thus observed scaling—and reported utilization—can differ from the raw ratio. Verify the resource metrics API, usually supplied by separately installed Metrics Server. Sources: `tasks/run-application/horizontal-pod-autoscale.md:109–202,433–486`.

7. **Least-privilege networking.** Select API and database Pods with default-deny ingress and egress policies, then explicitly allow approved API callers, API-to-database traffic on required ports, DNS resolution, and other demonstrated dependencies. Ingress and egress isolation are independent; selecting a Pod for one direction does not isolate the other. Policies combine additively: an overly broad allow cannot be overridden by another deny policy. Pod-to-Pod connections require permission at both source egress and destination ingress; replies to permitted connections are implicitly allowed. Verify DNS destinations and ports in this cluster. A policy-capable CNI is a prerequisite—otherwise resources can be ignored. Sources: `concepts/services-networking/network-policies.md:51–81`; `concepts/security/multi-tenancy.md:208–234`.

8. **Secrets.** Reject base64-encoded credentials in Git as a protection mechanism. The documentation demonstrates decoding and describes encoded credentials as obscured, not secret. Secrets are unencrypted in etcd by default; explicitly enable encryption at rest, configure least-privilege RBAC, and expose credentials only to containers requiring them. Encryption of cluster storage does not protect a readable Git manifest. Restrict workload-creation permissions too: permission to create Pods—or indirectly Deployments—in a namespace permits access to that namespace’s Secrets. Validate actual encrypted storage; the supplied procedure checks etcd ciphertext and successful API decryption. Sources: `concepts/configuration/secret.md:30–53,280–306`; `tasks/administer-cluster/encrypt-data.md:534–588`.

9. **Storage lifecycle.** StorageClass reclaim policy supplies the policy when dynamic PVs are created; do not treat changing or replacing the class definition as a retrofit for existing PVs. Inspect and explicitly patch existing PV policies when necessary. After PVC deletion releases a volume, `Retain` preserves the PV and data for manual reclamation; supported `Delete` implementations remove both PV and backing storage. Dynamic provisioning defaults to `Delete`. StatefulSet deletion or downscaling normally retains PVCs, a separate decision from PV reclamation. Its optional PVC-retention policy can delete claims on deletion or scale-down, which can then trigger PV reclamation. Confirm the documented feature-gate prerequisites before relying on that field. Sources: `concepts/storage/storage-classes.md:32–36,126–135`; `concepts/storage/persistent-volumes.md:178–210`; `concepts/workloads/controllers/statefulset.md:387–423`.

10. **Prioritized validation checklist.** First remove exposed credentials, verify encryption/RBAC and CNI enforcement, and inspect database PV reclamation and recovery procedures. Next establish resource requests, working metrics, startup/readiness/liveness behavior, TERM handling, and measured grace periods. Then deploy explicit spreading and the conservative rollout configuration with spare capacity; choose an HPA minimum preserving the intended six-replica baseline. Exercise drain, node loss, zone loss, slow startup, missing metrics, database interruption, and long requests during rollout. At least four limits remain unproven: request completion within grace, sufficient surviving-zone capacity, probe correctness under load, and database recovery/durability. Dynamic provisioning alone establishes none of those outcomes.
